Neon signs for business: logos, shopfronts and bars

A neon sign is one of the few pieces of branding people photograph on purpose. Done well, it tells passers-by you’re open, gives your counter a focal point and turns up in your customers’ photos. Here is how to get one that works.

      Your logo, in tube

      Send us your logo and a designer redraws it as neon, then sends a mockup and an exact price within 12 hours. Nothing is made until you approve the drawing.

      Vector files (SVG or PDF from a design program) give the cleanest start, but a clear PNG or a photo of a sketch works too. Very fine detail, thin outlines and gradients don’t translate directly into tube, so the designer simplifies where needed and shows you exactly how before anything is made.       What reads from the street

      • Fewer, bigger words. “Open”, your name, or one short line. From across the road, three words beat ten.
      • Letter height for distance. A sign maker’s rule of thumb is that every 2.5 cm of letter height reads from about 3 m. For a window seen from the pavement opposite, go Large or bigger.
      • Contrast with what’s behind. A busy shop interior behind a window sign eats the glow. A black rectangle backing gives the tube a clean background.
      • Hot colours travel. Red, pink and orange read from further away than blues.

      Our standard signs are made for indoors, including inside a shop window. For a sign outside the building, on a terrace or exposed to the weather, we quote an outdoor build separately.

      Behind a bar, above a counter

      Inside, a neon sign does its best work as a focal point: the wall behind the bar, the space above the coffee machine, the mirror wall in a salon. Here mood matters more than legibility, so script lettering and warm colours come into their own. Colour-changing tube lets a bar shift from daytime to late night from one remote.

      Where it works hardest

      Think about where people are when they see the sign. A window sign works for people walking past, so it needs to read in a glance. A sign behind the counter works for people queuing, so it can be more playful. A sign in a corner or by the door is for photographs, so it wants a good background and a spot where people can stand in front of it. Many businesses end up with two: a simple one in the window that says who you are, and a fun one inside that people share.

      Check the light, too. A sign in a sunny window competes with daylight and looks its best from late afternoon; a sign in a dim bar reads at any brightness, so you can run it lower with the dimmer.

      The practical bits

      • Power: every sign plugs into a normal socket with a UK plug. There’s no wiring, so no electrician is needed. Plan the cable route before it goes up.
      • Hanging: wall screws and standoffs for permanence, chains from a ceiling or window frame, or a stand for a counter.
      • Control: the dimmer and remote are included free. Many businesses plug the power supply into a timer so the sign comes on at opening.
      • Invoices: every order comes with an invoice for your records.

      Keeping it on brand

      A neon sign is a translation of your logo, not a copy. Tube has a fixed line weight, so thin and thick strokes in a logo become one consistent line, and filled shapes become outlines. Good neon logos keep what people recognise, the shape of the wordmark, the key symbol and the colours, and let go of the rest. Send your brand colours if you have them; we’ll match the nearest tube colours and name them on the drawing so there are no surprises.

      How to brief us

      1. Your logo or wording, in the best file you have.
      2. Where it’s going: inside, a window, or outside.
      3. Roughly how wide you’d like it, or a photo of the wall.
      4. Your colours, and whether you’d like a backing colour or a mirror finish.
      5. Any date you need it by.

      That’s enough for a designer to draw it. If something is unclear, they’ll ask rather than guess.
